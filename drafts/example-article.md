A digital garden is a publishing model, not a visual style. The interesting
part isn't the soft colours or the plant metaphors — it's that the unit of
work stops being *the post* and becomes *the claim*, and claims get revised in
place instead of being superseded by a newer post that quietly contradicts the
old one.

That single change cascades. It changes what you write, when you publish, and
what a reader is entitled to assume when they arrive.

## What changes when nothing is finished

A blog post is a dated artifact. It says: this is what I thought on this day,
and I am not coming back. That's an honest contract, and it's why blog
archives rot — the 2019 post is still the top search result for a thing the
author stopped believing in 2021.

A garden makes the opposite promise: the URL is stable, and what lives at it is
the current best version of the thought. The date that matters is not when it
was written but when it was last tended.

> The cost of that promise is that you have to actually go back. A garden
> nobody revisits is just a blog with confusing labels.

## Stages are a confidence signal

Every article here carries one of three stages, and they're the most useful
piece of metadata on the page:

![Three circles left to right: an outline circle labelled seedling, a half-filled circle labelled budding, and a solid filled circle labelled evergreen, connected by dotted lines.](/images/garden-stages.svg)

*The three stages, drawn the way they appear beside every article title.*

- **seedling** — a rough capture. I'm thinking out loud and I may be wrong.
- **budding** — a real position, argued, but still moving.
- **evergreen** — settled. Still tended, but I'd defend it.

I used to treat these as a ladder: every seedling was supposed to climb toward
evergreen, and anything stuck at seedling was a failure. That was wrong, and
fixing it is the single most useful revision this piece has had. A stage is not
a measure of effort spent. It's a claim about how much weight *you* should put
on the piece today. Plenty of ideas deserve to sit at seedling forever, and
saying so out loud is more useful than quietly polishing them into a confidence
they haven't earned.

## The changelog is the actual product

Here is the part that took me longest to see. In a garden, the most valuable
artifact isn't any individual article — it's the record of how a position
changed and why.

Anyone can tell you what they think. Very few people can show you what they
used to think, when they abandoned it, and what evidence moved them. That
record is hard to fake, it compounds, and it's the thing worth reading.

So the changelog on each article records changes *in the thinking*, not changes
in the prose. "Fixed typos" is noise. "Reversed the core claim after three
months of review data" is the signal — and it's why every article here also
publishes a history document alongside its source, linked from the meta line
at the top of the page.

This is also why [publishing is just a push](./tending-over-publishing.md):
any ceremony around publishing creates pressure to be finished, and being
finished is exactly the state a garden is trying to avoid.

## What I'm still unsure about

Whether stages survive contact with a reader who has never seen a garden
before. The vocabulary is inside baseball, and a first-time visitor arriving
from a search result has no reason to know that "seedling" means *discount
this accordingly*. I don't have a better answer yet — that's a
[legibility problem](./writing-for-strangers.md) I haven't solved.
