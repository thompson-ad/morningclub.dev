A blade is never finished. You take it off the stone when it's sharp enough for
the work in front of you, and you come back to it when it isn't. That is a
publishing model, and it's a better one than the essay — because the unit of
work stops being *the post* and becomes *the claim*, and claims get corrected in
place rather than quietly contradicted by a newer post.

That single change cascades. It changes what you write, when you publish, and
what a reader is entitled to assume when they arrive.

## What changes when nothing is finished

A blog post is a dated artefact. It says: this is what I thought on this day,
and I am not coming back. That's an honest contract, and it's why blog archives
rot — the 2019 post is still the top search result for a thing the author
stopped believing in 2021.

This makes the opposite promise: the URL is stable, and what lives at it is the
current best version of the thought. The date that matters is not when it was
written but when it was last worked.

> The cost of that promise is that you have to actually go back. A bench nobody
> returns to is just a blog with confusing labels.

## The stages are a report on the edge

Every article here carries one of three stages, and they're the most useful
piece of metadata on the page:

![Three circles left to right: an outline circle labelled rough, a half-filled circle labelled honed, and a solid filled circle labelled keen, connected by dotted lines.](/images/bench-stages.svg)

*The three stages, drawn the way they appear beside every article title.*

- **rough** — just off the saw. I'm thinking out loud and I may be wrong.
- **honed** — taking an edge. A real position, argued, but still moving.
- **keen** — sharp, and kept that way. I'd defend it.

I used to treat these as a ladder: everything rough was supposed to climb toward
keen, and anything still rough was a failure. That was wrong, and fixing it is
the single most useful pass this piece has had. A stage is not a measure of
effort spent. It's a claim about how much weight *you* should put on the piece
today. Plenty of ideas deserve to sit at rough forever, and saying so out loud is
more useful than quietly polishing them into a confidence they haven't earned.

## The changelog is the actual product

Here is the part that took me longest to see. The most valuable artefact isn't
any individual article — it's the record of how a position changed and why.

Anyone can tell you what they think. Very few people can show you what they used
to think, when they abandoned it, and what evidence moved them. That record is
hard to fake, it compounds, and it's the thing worth reading.

So the changelog on each article records changes *in the thinking*, not changes
in the prose. "Fixed typos" is noise. "Reversed the core claim after three
months of data" is the signal — and it's why every article here also publishes a
history document alongside its source, linked from the meta line at the top.

This is also why [publishing is just a push](./honing-over-publishing.md): any
ceremony around publishing creates pressure to be finished, and being finished is
exactly the state this is trying to avoid.

## What I'm still unsure about

Whether the vocabulary survives contact with a reader who has never seen it
before. "Rough" and "keen" are legible enough, but a first-time visitor arriving
from a search result has no particular reason to know that rough means *discount
this accordingly*. I don't have a better answer yet — that's a
[legibility problem](./writing-for-strangers.md) I haven't solved.
